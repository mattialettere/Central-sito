Elderly parent living alone: close, without controlling

Many adult children live far from their parents. The worry is real, and so is the independence of the person living alone. Here's how to find a balance, with or without technology.

Updated on 24 November 2026

In short: a simple, agreed routine works better than constant monitoring. A call or message at a set time, an "I'm OK" check-in that only alerts you if the confirmation is missing, a neighbour to rely on and up-to-date emergency contacts cover most situations without taking away independence.

The starting point: ask, don't impose

A parent who lives alone often values their independence a lot. Suggesting a tracking app "for peace of mind" can sound like distrust. It is better to start with a question: what would make them feel safer? Sometimes the answer is a neighbour with a spare key, sometimes a morning phone call, sometimes a button to ask for help.

Whatever you choose, it should be something your parent accepts, understands and can switch off.

The options, simplest first

1. A contact routine

A call or message at a set time, every day. It is the simplest solution and often the most welcome, because it is also a moment to talk. The limit: it relies on both of you remembering, and if one day it's missed, it isn't clear whether to worry.

2. A check-in that only alerts you if it's missed

A check-in turns the logic around: your parent confirms with one tap that they are fine by an agreed time. If the confirmation arrives, nothing happens. If it doesn't, you get an alert. No location sharing is needed and nobody watches a map.

In Central, the "I'm OK" check-in can have different times for each day, sends a reminder before the deadline, and the check runs on the server, so the alert goes out even if the phone is off or the battery is flat.

3. A quick way to ask for help

An SOS button is only useful if it can be used when needed. For an older person, simplicity matters: a voice command ("Hey Siri, Central SOS" on iPhone) or a Quick Settings tile on Android can be easier than opening an app. Try it together, calmly, before it's needed.

4. People nearby

Technology sends the alert, but someone has to be able to get there. A neighbour, friend or relative who lives close by and has a key is still the most valuable resource. Add them to the emergency contacts.

5. Medical information for first responders

Allergies, medications and conditions in the iPhone Medical ID or Android emergency information help first responders even when the phone is locked.

What about continuous location tracking?

Always knowing where a parent is may seem reassuring, but it rarely answers the real question, which is "are they OK?". A dot on a map doesn't tell you whether someone has fallen at home. That is why a check-in or an SOS is often more useful than a map that is always on. If you need to know they arrived at a medical appointment, share the location just for that journey, as Safe Trip does, and then stop.

When more is needed

If your parent has memory problems, frequent falls or conditions that need care, an app is not enough. Their doctor and local social services can point you to suitable personal alarm (telecare) and home care services.
