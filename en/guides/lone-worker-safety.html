Lone worker safety: protecting people who work alone

Cleaning, security, home care, maintenance: many people work with no colleagues around. Here's how to organise contact and alarms so nobody is alone in an emergency.

Download for iPhone Download for Android

Updated on 25 November 2026

In short: working alone is not banned, but it needs three things: a risk assessment that looks specifically at lone working, an agreed way to keep in touch (set times or check-ins) and a way to raise the alarm that someone actually receives. Test the tools in advance, together with the people who will use them.

Who counts as a lone worker

The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) defines lone workers as those who work by themselves without close or direct supervision. Its examples are very practical: cleaners, security staff, home care and health workers, engineers, delivery drivers, people working in warehouses or petrol stations, and people working from home.

The HSE notes that there will always be greater risks for lone workers, because there is nobody around to help if things go wrong. The problem is often not the job itself but how long it takes before anyone notices.

What the law says

In Great Britain, the HSE explains that under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations employers must manage the risks to lone workers and include them in their general risk assessment, before people work alone. This also covers contractors and self-employed people.

In Italy the reference is the consolidated health and safety law, D.Lgs. 81/2008, where lone working belongs in the risk assessment (art. 28) and in emergency and first aid planning. Other countries have their own rules: check with your health and safety adviser. This guide is not legal advice.

Good practice in five steps

1. Assess the risks of working alone

According to the HSE, risks that particularly affect lone workers include violence, stress and mental wellbeing, a person's medical suitability to work alone, and the workplace itself, for example if it is rural or isolated.

2. Set limits

Some work should never be done alone. The HSE says certain high-risk work requires at least one other person, for example in confined spaces or near exposed live electrical conductors. For everything else, agree what can and can't be done alone.

3. Keep in touch

The HSE says employers must monitor lone workers and keep in touch with them: pre-agreed intervals of regular contact, devices for raising the alarm, and a reliable system to make sure someone has returned to base. The higher the risk, the more supervision is needed.

4. An alarm that someone receives

A panic button only helps if a person on the other end gets the alert, knows where to go and what to do. Decide who receives alarms at every hour, including nights and weekends.

5. Test and train

The HSE recommends regularly testing systems and emergency procedures, and training people to use the tools. A monthly test run avoids surprises on the day it matters.

Digital tools: what to ask

  • Quick SOS: can it be started in seconds, even with the phone locked?
  • Check-ins: if the worker doesn't confirm on time, who gets the alert? Does it work even if the phone is off or the battery is flat?
  • Location: is it shared only during an emergency or a journey, or all the time? Continuous tracking of employees raises privacy issues and needs careful thought.
  • Coordination: is there a dashboard for the office or control room, with an alert history?
