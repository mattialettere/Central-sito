What to say in an emergency, and to whom

In a panic, the simplest things are the first to go. This checklist separates what emergency services need from what the people you trust need.

Updated on 24 November 2026

In short: emergency services need where you are, what happened, how many people are involved and how they are; then stay on the line and answer their questions. The people you trust need your location, how you are, whether you can talk and what they should do. If you are in immediate danger, your first call always goes to emergency services.

Emergency services first

112 is the European emergency number, available in every EU country. An operator answers and passes the call to the service you need: ambulance, police or fire brigade. The EU's Your Europe page on 112 reminds you that it is only for emergencies, not for general information. Outside the EU the number may differ: in the UK both 999 and 112 work, and in the US and Canada it is 911.

What to tell the operator

  1. Where you are. Street address with house number, town, floor. If you don't know: landmarks, the nearest street name, the road marker. Modern smartphones send their location to emergency services where the feature is active, but don't rely on that alone.
  2. What happened. In one sentence: "a man has fallen and can't get up", "there's smoke coming from the garage".
  3. How many people and how they are. Are they conscious? Breathing? Bleeding?
  4. Your phone number, if the operator asks, so they can call you back.

Then stay on the line and follow the instructions: the operator can guide you through first steps. Don't hang up first.

If you can't speak

Some public emergency apps offer a silent call or a chat with the control room where the service is active; in Italy, for example, 112 Where ARE U. It is worth checking in advance what is available where you live.

What to send the people you trust

Family and friends don't replace emergency services, but they can come to you, bring documents or medicines, tell others and talk to doctors. They need different information:

  • Your exact location, ideally on a map.
  • How you are and whether you have already called emergency services.
  • Whether you can talk or whether it's better to text.
  • Your phone battery: if it's about to die, whoever is looking for you needs to know.
  • What they should do: come over, call someone, wait for news.

Typing all this while you are upset is hard. That is why SOS apps collect part of the information for you. In Central, SOS sends your emergency contacts your location and battery and, if started from the app, a 30-second video showing the situation, together with a prompt to call 112.

A checklist to keep in mind

Where you areWhere you are, on a map
What happenedHow you are, whether you called for help

Stay on the lineBattery and what they should do

Prepare in advance

  • Set up emergency contacts on your phone and tell them.
  • Give location permission to the apps that need it in an emergency.
  • Teach children their address and how to call the emergency number.

This guide gives general information and does not replace the instructions of the emergency operator, which should always be followed.
