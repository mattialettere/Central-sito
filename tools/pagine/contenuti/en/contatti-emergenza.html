In short: pick two or three people who are reachable, nearby and calm, and tell them. Then save them in three places: in the iPhone Medical ID or Android emergency information (which first responders can see even when the phone is locked), in your contacts with a recognisable name and, if you want an alert with your location, in an SOS app.

Who to choose as an emergency contact

Your emergency contact is not necessarily the person you are closest to. It is the person who, once alerted, can actually do something. A few useful criteria:

  • Reachable: answers the phone in the evening and doesn't keep it on silent all the time.
  • Nearby: at least one of your contacts should be able to get to you quickly.
  • Informed: knows your address, any health issues and who else to tell.
  • Calm: in an emergency you need someone who calls for help instead of panicking.

Two or three people are enough. If you choose only one, think about what happens when they are travelling, in a meeting or have a flat battery.

Tell them, and agree on what to do

An emergency contact who doesn't know they are one is not much use. Talk about it beforehand: what should they do if they get an alert? Call you, call emergency services, come over, tell someone else? A simple agreement is enough, for example: "if you get an SOS and I don't answer within two minutes, call 112 and give them my address".

How to set them up on your phone

iPhone: Medical ID

In the Health app you can fill in your Medical ID with allergies, medications, blood type and emergency contacts. With "Show When Locked" turned on, first responders can see it from the lock screen without your passcode. With "Share During Emergency Call", your iPhone sends it to emergency services when you call them, where the feature is available.

Android: emergency information

On many Android phones, emergency information (medical details and contacts) is set up in the safety settings or, on Pixel phones, in the Personal Safety app. You can make it visible from the lock screen. Google points out that, in that case, anyone who picks up your phone can read it: only write what is really needed.

In your contacts

An old but useful habit: save your contacts with names that say who they are, such as "Mum" or "Mark (brother)". Anyone who finds your phone or helps you knows straight away who to call.

What an SOS app adds

Medical ID and emergency information help the people next to you. They don't alert the people who are far away. An SOS app does the opposite: it sends an alert to the people you trust, wherever they are, with the information they need to act.

In Central, for example, SOS sends your emergency contacts your location, battery level and, if it is started from the app, a 30-second video, all end-to-end encrypted. You can also start it with Siri on iPhone or from the Quick Settings tile on Android. The free plan lets you choose 2 emergency contacts. Each contact is notified when you add them, so nobody receives an SOS without knowing why.

Some countries also have public apps linked to emergency services. In several Italian regions, for example, the 112 Where ARE U app calls 112 where available and sends your location to the control room. It has a different purpose: calling for help, not alerting your family.

Check every now and then

  • Once a year, check that numbers and people are still right.
  • When you change phone, check your Medical ID or emergency information again: it doesn't always transfer by itself.
  • Try the SOS app with a contact you have warned in advance, so you know what the alert looks like on the other side.
